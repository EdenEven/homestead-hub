# Raising Backyard Chickens for Eggs & Meat: Beginner's Guide

*Everything you need to know to start your first backyard flock — from choosing breeds to butcher day — without the rookie mistakes.*

There's a reason chickens are the gateway animal for almost every homesteader. They're forgiving, productive, and genuinely entertaining to keep. A small flock of six hens can supply a family with fresh eggs year-round, and a handful of dual-purpose or meat birds can put real food in your freezer by fall. If you've been on the fence about getting started, this guide will walk you through everything from brooder setup to butcher day.

## Choosing the Right Breed for Your Goals

Not all chickens are built the same, and your breed choice matters more than most beginners realize.

**For eggs:** The Leghorn is a laying machine — expect 280–320 white eggs per year per bird. Rhode Island Reds and Plymouth Rocks are workhorses that lay consistently and handle cold winters well. If you want a calmer bird that's easier around kids, Black Australorps are hard to beat and hold the world record for egg production (364 eggs in 365 days from one hen, recorded by the Australian Poultry Journal).

**For meat:** The Cornish Cross is the industry standard for a reason — it reaches butcher weight (5–7 lbs) in just 6–8 weeks. They're not glamorous and require close management, but the feed-to-meat conversion is exceptional. Freedom Rangers are a slower-growing alternative (10–12 weeks) with better foraging ability and flavor.

**For both (dual-purpose):** Buckeyes, Dominiques, and Delaware chickens offer a solid middle ground — decent egg production and a respectable carcass. They're the right choice if you want a self-sustaining flock that can do it all.

## Setting Up Your Brooder

Chicks arrive fragile and need warmth above everything else. A basic brooder can be built from a large plastic storage tote or a cardboard box lined with pine shavings (never cedar — the oils are toxic to poultry).

- **Temperature:** Start at 95°F directly under the heat source for the first week, then drop by 5°F each week until fully feathered (around 6 weeks). A simple 250-watt heat lamp or a radiant brooder plate like the Brinsea EcoGlow works well.
- **Space:** Plan for at least 0.5 square feet per chick in the brooder, more as they grow.
- **Water and feed:** Use chick-specific starter feed (18–20% protein) from day one. Medicated starter feed helps protect against coccidiosis, a common and deadly protozoan infection in young birds.
- **Watch their behavior:** Chicks huddled directly under the lamp are cold. Chicks pressed against the walls away from the heat are too hot. A comfortable, evenly spread flock means you've got the temperature right.

## Building a Coop and Run That Actually Works

The single biggest mistake beginners make is underbuilding their coop. Plan for more space than you think you need — the rule of thumb is 4 square feet per bird inside the coop and 10 square feet per bird in the outdoor run. Crowding leads to pecking, stress, and disease.

**Key coop features:**
- Ventilation near the roofline (not drafts at bird level)
- Roosting bars at least 2 inches wide — chickens sleep flat-footed, not gripping like songbirds
- One nest box per 4–5 hens, positioned lower than the roost so birds don't sleep in them
- Easy-clean flooring — the deep litter method using straw or wood shavings builds beneficial microbes and reduces odor naturally

**Predator protection is non-negotiable.** Raccoons can open simple latches. Foxes dig. Weasels squeeze through gaps smaller than an inch. Use hardware cloth (not chicken wire, which is flimsy) with a 1/2-inch mesh. Bury an apron of hardware cloth 12 inches outward from your run perimeter to stop diggers. A heavy-duty sliding bolt or carabiner on every door is cheap insurance.

## Feeding, Water, and Flock Health

Layering hens need a complete layer feed (16% protein, with added calcium) once they start producing around 18–20 weeks of age. Offer crushed oyster shell free-choice on the side — strong shells require more calcium than layer feed alone provides.

Meat birds like Cornish Cross need a high-protein broiler feed (22–24%) and continuous access to water. Restrict feed at night for Cornish Cross after week 3 to prevent leg problems from growing too fast.

Fresh, clean water every day is non-negotiable. Dirty water is a fast path to respiratory illness and coccidiosis. Nipple waterers stay cleaner than open troughs and are worth the small investment.

**Biosecurity basics every beginner needs to follow:**
- Quarantine any new birds for 30 days before introducing them to your flock
- Change footwear before entering your coop if you've visited other farms or feed stores
- Remove and compost or bury dead birds promptly — never leave them in the run
- Know the signs of common illness: labored breathing, swollen eyes, bloody droppings, sudden drop in egg production. Your state's cooperative extension service (like those listed through USDA NRCS) can help with local disease alerts and free flock health resources

## Egg Collection and Processing

Collect eggs at least once a day — twice in summer heat. Unwashed eggs have a natural protective coating called the bloom that keeps them fresh at room temperature for up to two weeks. Once washed, refrigerate immediately and use within a month.

For meat birds, processing day comes around week 6–8 for Cornish Cross. If you're doing your own butchering, a killing cone, sharp boning knife, and a large pot for scalding (145–150°F for 30–60 seconds) are your basic tools. The Merck Veterinary Manual and Harvey Ussery's *The Small-Scale Poultry Flock* are both excellent references for the full process. If you'd rather have someone else handle it, many small-scale USDA-inspected processors take farm birds — search your county extension office for listings.

## Common Beginner Mistakes to Avoid

- **Buying too many birds too fast.** Start with 4–6 hens. Learn the rhythm before scaling up.
- **Skipping the hardware cloth.** Chicken wire keeps chickens in. It does not keep predators out.
- **Ignoring the rooster laws.** Many municipalities ban roosters due to noise ordinances. Check local zoning before you buy straight-run chicks.
- **Not having a plan for extra roosters.** If you hatch your own or buy straight-run, roughly half will be male. Know before you start whether you'll cull, rehome, or process them.
